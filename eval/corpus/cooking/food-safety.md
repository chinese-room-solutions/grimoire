# Food safety

The rules worth actually keeping, separated from the ones that exist because a
regulator had to write something enforceable for a kitchen with staff turnover.

## Temperature and time

The danger zone is 4 to 60 °C, and the number that matters is not the
temperature alone but how long the food spends there. Four hours cumulative is
the working limit — cumulative across prep, transport and service, not four
hours per step.

Cooking is also time and temperature together. The CDC and the food agencies
publish single numbers because a single number is teachable, but chicken held at
60 °C for half an hour is as safe as chicken taken instantly to 74 °C, and it is
a far better piece of chicken. The pasteurisation tables are the honest version;
the single number is the poster.

Cooling is the step people get wrong. Hot stock left in a deep pot spends six
hours in the zone. Shallow pans, an ice bath, or a frozen bottle stirred through
it: 60 to 20 °C in two hours, 20 to 4 °C in four more.

## Where the actual risk is

- Rice held warm. *Bacillus cereus* spores survive cooking and its toxin
  survives reheating, so the reheat does not save you. Cool it fast or throw it
  out.
- Garlic in oil at room temperature. Anaerobic, low acid, and the one home
  preparation with a real botulism history. Refrigerate it and use it within
  the week, or acidify it.
- Cross-contamination from raw poultry, which is mostly a matter of what the
  hands touch between the bird and the tap — see [[knife-skills]] for how the
  board gets organised.
- Reheating rather than cooking: bring leftovers to a genuine simmer, not warm.

Rinsing raw chicken does nothing except aerosolise the problem across the sink.

## Fermentation is not an exception

It looks like a violation of every rule above and it is not: salt and the acid
the culture produces do the work that refrigeration does elsewhere. A brine at
2% and a pH under 4.6 is a hostile place for anything that would make you ill,
which is why the salt ratio is the one measurement in the whole process that is
not a matter of taste; see [[fermentation]] and [[sourdough]].

Mould on the surface of a ferment is a surface problem — lift it off and check
the smell. Pink or fuzzy growth through the body of it is not, and the batch
goes out.

## The bit nobody follows

Label everything with the date it was made. Not the date it expires — the date
it was made, because that is the only fact you know at the time, and a fridge
full of undated containers becomes a fridge you throw out.
