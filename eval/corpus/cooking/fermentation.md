# Fermentation

Notes on the vegetable and brine side of things. The bread culture has its own
page, [[sourdough]].

## Why it is safe

Lactic acid bacteria are already on the vegetables. Submerged in brine with no
oxygen, they out-compete everything else, and as they work they drop the pH
below 4.6, which is the line below which *Clostridium botulinum* cannot grow.
The process makes itself safe as it proceeds; the risk window is the first day
or two, before the acid has built up.

Two rules follow, and they are the whole practice:

1. **Everything stays under the brine.** A weight, a cabbage leaf, a water-filled
   bag. What is exposed to air grows mould.
2. **Salt is not optional.** 2% by weight of vegetable plus water is the
   standard, 3.5% for a brine-covered pickle. Below about 1.5% the wrong
   organisms get a chance; above 5% the lactic bacteria themselves are inhibited
   and it just gets salty.

Kahm yeast is a flat white wrinkled film. Harmless, tastes musty, skim it off.
Fuzzy or coloured growth is mould: discard the batch. Slimy brine usually means
the temperature was too high early on.

## Temperature

The single biggest lever, and the reason two identical jars behave differently.

- **18-22C** is the sweet spot for sauerkraut and most vegetables. Complex,
  clean, predictable. Ready in two to four weeks.
- **Above 26C** the fermentation runs fast and rough — soft texture, harsher
  acidity, more risk in the early days before the pH drops.
- **Below 15C** it crawls. Not a problem, just slow, and the flavour tends to be
  cleaner and less complex.
- **Around 4C**, the fridge, it effectively stops. That is how you hold a batch
  at the sourness you like.

Warm start, cool finish is the technique worth knowing: two days at room
temperature to get the population going, then somewhere cool for the long
development. Same logic as a cold retard in bread — get the biology started
warm, then slow it down so the slower flavour reactions can catch up with the
gas production.

Temperature is also why summer batches and winter batches from the same recipe
are not the same food. Write the room temperature on the jar label with the
date.

## Standards

**Sauerkraut.** Shred cabbage, 2% salt by weight, massage until it releases
enough liquid to submerge itself, pack tight, weight it. Three weeks at 20C.

**Kimchi.** Brine the cabbage separately at 5% for two hours, rinse, then coat
with the paste (gochugaru, garlic, ginger, fish sauce, a little sugar or rice
porridge to feed the bacteria). Two days out, then the fridge. It keeps
developing for months and gets better for stews as it goes.

**Brined pickles.** 3.5% brine to cover, plus tannin — a grape leaf, a black tea
bag — or cucumbers go soft as their own pectinase works on them. Garlic, dill,
mustard seed.

**Hot sauce.** Chillies and 2.5% brine, two weeks, then blend with a splash of
the brine and vinegar to stabilize. Ferment first, blend after; the reverse
ferments unpredictably because there is no intact surface for the bacteria to
work from.

## Kit

Wide-mouth jars, glass weights, and airlock lids that let CO2 out without
letting air in. Airlocks are convenience rather than necessity — burping a
sealed jar daily works, and forgetting to burp one is how a cupboard gets
repainted.

A pH strip settles arguments. Under 4.0 and it is preserved, whatever it looks
like.
