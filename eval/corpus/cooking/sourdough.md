# Sourdough

## The culture

A jar of flour and water hosting wild yeast and lactic acid bacteria. The yeast
makes the gas, the bacteria make the acid and most of the flavour. They are in a
stable relationship at the pH the bacteria create, which is why a mature culture
is hard to spoil and a three-day-old one is not.

Maintenance, once it is established:

- **On the counter**, feed twice a day at 1:5:5 (one part culture, five flour,
  five water by weight) at around 22C. It peaks in 6-8 hours and is usable for
  a few hours either side.
- **In the fridge**, feed once a week. Take it out, feed, leave two hours until
  it is visibly active, then put it back. Cold slows everything to a crawl;
  the population survives for months this way.
- **Neglected**, it separates and grows a layer of grey liquid on top. That is
  alcohol, it means hungry, not dead. Pour it off, discard all but a spoonful,
  feed at 1:10:10 twice a day, and it comes back in two or three days.

What actually kills it: mould (fuzzy, coloured, anything not white — throw the
jar out, no scraping), a genuinely dried-out crust with nothing living
underneath, or heat above about 45C. Almost everything else is recoverable.
People kill far more cultures by panicking and over-diluting than by neglect.

Rye is the reliable rescue flour. A hungry culture fed 50% rye usually doubles
within twelve hours when white flour alone would not.

Whether it needs a name is between you and the jar. Mine does not.

## Basic loaf

800g strong white, 200g wholemeal, 750g water (75%), 200g leaven, 20g salt.

1. **Autolyse.** Flour and 700g of the water, mixed to no dry patches, rested an
   hour. Hydration and time do the gluten development that kneading would
   otherwise have to.
2. **Mix.** Add the leaven, then the salt with the last 50g of water. Squeeze
   through until homogeneous.
3. **Bulk.** Four sets of stretch and folds at 30 minute intervals, then leave
   it. Done when it has risen 50%, feels airy, and shows bubbles at the edges of
   the container. Roughly 4-5 hours at 24C, and much longer if the kitchen is
   cold — the clock is a suggestion and the dough is the instruction. General
   temperature effects in [[fermentation#Temperature]].
4. **Preshape**, bench rest 30 minutes, **final shape** with tension across the
   surface.
5. **Cold retard.** Banneton, covered, fridge overnight, 12-16 hours. This is
   where the sour comes from, because the bacteria keep working when the yeast
   has mostly stopped. It also makes a cold, firm dough that scores cleanly.
6. **Bake** straight from the fridge in a preheated Dutch oven: 250C lid on for
   20 minutes, then 230C lid off for 20-25 until it is properly dark.

Cool for at least two hours. Cutting hot bread gums the crumb; the starch is
still setting.

## Diagnosis

- **Dense and gummy** — under-fermented, or cut too early. Extend the bulk.
- **Flat, spreading, no oven spring** — over-fermented. The gluten has been
  degraded by protease and no shaping recovers it. Shorten the bulk or use less
  leaven.
- **No ear** — the score was too shallow or not angled. One decisive cut at 30
  degrees, a scalpel-sharp blade.
- **Big irregular holes near the top, dense base** — poor shaping, gas migrating
  up through a weak structure.
- **Pale crust** — not hot enough, not long enough, or all the sugar was
  consumed during too long a bulk.

## Notes to self

Weigh everything, including the water. Track dough temperature, not room
temperature — the friction of mixing adds a couple of degrees and it is the
variable that explains most inconsistency between two loaves made the same way.
