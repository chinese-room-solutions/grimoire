# Hydration and baker's percentages

Bread recipes written in grams are a snapshot; recipes written in percentages
are a formula you can scale and adapt. The convention: flour is always 100,
and everything else is a percentage of the flour *by weight*.

```
750g water / 1000g flour = 75% hydration
20g salt  / 1000g flour = 2% salt
```

Salt at 1.8-2.2%, yeast around 0.2-1% fresh depending on timeline, and
leaven whatever the recipe says — the number that varies across breads, and
the one that drives how the dough behaves, is hydration.

## What the numbers do

| Hydration | Dough | Bread |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 55-60% | stiff, kneads cleanly | bagels, pretzels: dense chew, tight crumb |
| 65% | tacky, workable | sandwich loaves, most everyday bread |
| 70-75% | soft, needs the fold techniques | open crumb, the sourdough default |
| 80%+ | slack, flows | ciabatta, focaccia: irregular holes, crisp crust |

The basic loaf in [[sourdough#Basic loaf]] is 75% — chosen because it is
about as wet as a dough that a normal person can shape in a home kitchen
without a scraper and a sense of humour.

Hydration is not just water added: it is water *relative to the flour's
capacity*, and the flour changes the denominator's behaviour. Wholemeal
absorbs more (the bran cuts gluten and soaks water), so an 80% wholemeal
dough handles like a 72% white one — add 5-10% water when swapping in
wholemeal, or the dough that was airy at 75% white comes out dense and
brick-like. Freshly milled flour is thirstier still. This is the single most
common reason "the same recipe" behaves differently between two kitchens or
two bags.

Rye has little gluten to hold water in a structure at all; high-rye doughs
are batter, not dough, and are baked in tins for that reason.

## Working wet dough

High hydration buys an open crumb and a custardy interior, and charges for
it in handling. The techniques that make 78% manageable:

- **Autolyse** — flour and water rest before anything else is added. Time
  does the gluten development kneading would have to force, and the dough is
  noticeably smoother for the rest of the process.
- **Stretch and folds** instead of kneading: four sets at 30 minute
  intervals during bulk, per the basic loaf. Each set is a minute of work;
  the dough transforms between them.
- **Wet hands and a bench knife** for shaping. Flouring the work surface
  fights the hydration you chose; water or oil lubricates without drying
  the surface.
- **Pre-shape and bench rest** matter more as hydration rises — 30 covered
  minutes lets the dough relax enough to take a final shape without
  tearing.
- **Cold retard** as the great equalifier: fridge overnight and even an 80%
  dough scores cleanly, because viscosity climbs as temperature drops. The
  scheduling logic in [[fermentation#Temperature]] is the same one.

## Diagnosis, hydration edition

- **Dense crumb, small holes evenly distributed** — under-hydrated for the
  flour used, or under-fermented; check which by whether it also tastes
  flat.
- **Flat, spread out, no shape** — over-hydrated for your shaping or your
  flour's protein. Drop 5% next time; not every flour carries 78%.
- **Sticky forever, never smooth** — the wholemeal adjustment was missed,
  or the salt was forgotten (salt tightens; its absence is felt in the
  texture before the taste).
- **Gummy interior, sounds hollow when cool** — cut too early, always; or
  genuinely underbaked. Two hours of cooling minimum, per
  [[sourdough#Basic loaf]].

Weigh everything, including water in millilitres that everyone "knows" is
grams. A measuring cup of flour varies 20% by scooping style, which is 20%
hydration error before anything else in the formula has a chance to matter.
